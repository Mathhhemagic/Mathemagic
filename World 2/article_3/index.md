:::title
Coordinate Plane
:::

The coordinate plane is a grid formed by two perpendicular number lines: the horizontal **x-axis** and the vertical **y-axis**. Their intersection is called the **origin**, located at (0, 0).

:::mini-title
Plotting Points
:::

Every point on the coordinate plane is written as an ordered pair (x, y). The x-value tells you how far to move left or right, and the y-value tells you how far to move up or down.

:::example
Point (3, 2)
Move 3 units right from the origin, then 2 units up.
:::

:::example
Point (−4, 1)
Move 4 units left from the origin, then 1 unit up.
:::

:::tip
Always move horizontally (x) first, then vertically (y).
:::

:::mini-title
Quadrants
:::

The x-axis and y-axis divide the plane into four **quadrants**, numbered counterclockwise starting from the top right.

Quadrant I: x is positive, y is positive.

Quadrant II: x is negative, y is positive.

Quadrant III: x is negative, y is negative.

Quadrant IV: x is positive, y is negative.

:::example
The point (5, −3) is in Quadrant IV, since x is positive and y is negative.
:::

:::mini-title
Distance Intuition
:::

The distance between two points is just how far apart they are on the plane.

If two points share the same y-value, the distance between them is simply the difference between their x-values.

:::example
(2, 4) and (7, 4)
Distance = 7 − 2 = 5
:::

If two points share the same x-value, the distance is the difference between their y-values.

:::example
(3, 1) and (3, 6)
Distance = 6 − 1 = 5
:::

When points don't share an x- or y-value, picture a right triangle connecting the two points, with the horizontal and vertical distances as the two legs. The distance between the points is the hypotenuse.

:::tip
You can find the horizontal and vertical legs by subtracting the x-values and the y-values separately, then use the Pythagorean theorem to find the actual distance.
:::

:::mini-title
Midpoint Intuition
:::

The **midpoint** of two points is the point exactly halfway between them.

To find it, average the x-values and average the y-values separately.

:::example
(2, 4) and (8, 10)
Midpoint = ((2 + 8)/2, (4 + 10)/2) = (5, 7)
:::

:::example
(−3, 6) and (5, −2)
Midpoint = ((−3 + 5)/2, (6 + (−2))/2) = (1, 2)
:::

:::tip
The midpoint is just the average of the x's and the average of the y's—nothing more.
:::
